# The Cost of Inefficiency

My plan for this post was ambitious. The note said: "Quantify the billions of dollars lost annually because we cannot perfectly solve NP problems." I imagined a big, dramatic number at the top of the page.

I spent most of a week looking for it. I found plenty of articles that **claim** big numbers. When I tried to trace those numbers back to an actual calculation, they either disappeared into vague estimates or turned out to be about something else entirely, like the value of using optimisation software at all.

So this post doesn't have a big number. Instead, it explains why that number can't honestly exist, what **can** be measured, and what the real cost of NP-Hardness looks like. I think that's more useful than a made-up figure, and it fits the spirit of this whole project: if I don't know something, I'd rather say so.

---

## Why the Number Can't Be Calculated

To know how much is lost by not solving a problem perfectly, you need two things:

1. what your current solution costs, and
2. what the **perfect** solution would cost.

The first is easy. The second is the whole problem. If you knew the cost of the perfect solution for every routing, scheduling and packing problem in the world, you would, in effect, have solved them. For NP-Hard problems at real-world scale, that's exactly what we can't do.

So "the amount lost because we can't solve NP-Hard problems" is a number that would require solving NP-Hard problems to compute. It's not just unknown. It's unknown **for the same reason** the problems are hard.

---

## What Can Be Measured: The Gap

But we're not completely in the dark. There's a clever partial answer, and it's what professional optimisation solvers report every time they run.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="relax" label="1. Relax the problem" default>

Take a hard problem and remove the rule that makes it hard. For example, in knapsack or crew scheduling, the hard part is that every decision is **yes or no**: take the item or don't, fly the pairing or don't.

Allow fractions instead: take 0.4 of an item, fly 0.7 of a pairing. That's physically meaningless, but the relaxed problem can be solved quickly, using linear programming.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="bound" label="2. Get a bound">

The relaxed problem has **more** freedom than the real one, so its best answer is at least as good as the real best answer.

So it gives a **bound**: "the true optimum can't be better than this". For a cost you're minimising, it's a floor that no real solution can go below.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="gap" label="3. Measure the gap">

Now compare your actual solution with the bound. The difference is called the **optimality gap**.

If your schedule costs 102 and the bound says nothing can cost less than 100, then you know you're within 2% of optimal, **without ever finding the optimum**. The perfect answer is somewhere between 100 and 102.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

This is how serious optimisation works in practice. Solvers keep improving the solution from above and tightening the bound from below, and stop when the gap is small enough. Post 69 is about the simplest example of this idea: the fractional version of knapsack.

So for a **specific** company's **specific** problem, you can often say "we're within 1% of perfect", and therefore "at most 1% of this cost is being lost". What you can't do is add that up across the whole world.

---

## Where the Real Costs Are

Here's what surprised me most: for many large, well-run operations, the gap between a good solver's answer and the optimum is small, often a few percent or less. The bigger losses usually come from elsewhere.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="notoptimising" label="Not optimising at all" default>

Many organisations still plan routes, shifts or stock levels by hand, with spreadsheets and experience. The difference between that and a decent heuristic can be far larger than the difference between a decent heuristic and the true optimum. Post 54's nearest-neighbour rule was 17–18% worse than optimal on our tiny example; a good solver would usually be within a fraction of a percent.

So a lot of the "cost of NP-Hardness" is really the cost of not using the tools that already exist.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="uncertainty" label="Uncertainty">

The perfect schedule for the weather forecast is useless if the forecast is wrong. Demand changes, drivers get sick, flights get delayed (Post 55). A plan that was optimal on Monday's data might be bad by Tuesday.

Optimising under uncertainty is much harder than optimising with known data, and it's often where the biggest real losses happen.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="speed" label="Running out of time">

Some decisions must be made in seconds: placing a job on a server (Post 56), re-routing a delivery around traffic, re-crewing a cancelled flight. There's no time to run a solver for an hour. The cost there is the gap between what you can compute **right now** and what you could compute with more time.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="modelling" label="Wrong model">

Every optimisation needs a description of the problem: the costs, the rules, what "better" means. If that description is wrong (missing a rule, a wrong cost, an outdated assumption), then even a perfect solution to the model is a bad solution to reality.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## So What Is the Cost of NP-Hardness?

Here's how I'd answer it now, honestly:

1. **Unmeasurable in total**, because measuring it requires knowing optimal solutions we can't compute.
2. **Measurable instance by instance**, as an optimality gap, using bounds from relaxed problems.
3. **Often small** for well-modelled problems with good solvers, a few percent or less, but multiplied by enormous operations, a few percent can be a lot of money.
4. **Easily confused** with other costs that are often larger: not optimising at all, uncertainty, time pressure and bad models.

And there's one more cost that doesn't show up on any balance sheet: the cost of **not knowing**. Without a proof of optimality, every planner lives with the question "could this be better?". The optimality gap gives a partial answer. P vs NP is the reason that's the best we can generally do.

:::note What We Have Established
The total money lost to NP-Hardness can't honestly be calculated, because doing so would require knowing the optimal solutions that NP-Hardness prevents us from computing.

For a specific problem, a **relaxation** (for example, allowing fractions instead of yes/no decisions) gives a **bound** on the optimum, and the **optimality gap** between your solution and the bound limits how much you could possibly be losing. Good solvers often achieve gaps of a few percent or less.

In practice, larger losses often come from not optimising at all, from uncertainty, from time pressure and from wrong models, not from the last few percent that NP-Hardness withholds.
:::
