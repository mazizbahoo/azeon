# Tautologies and coNP-Completeness

In my first-year logic lectures, we spent a whole week drawing truth tables. Rows and rows of T and F, for formulas with names like "De Morgan's law" and "the law of the excluded middle". At the time I thought it was the most boring thing I'd ever been asked to do.

Then I started this phase and found out that those boring tables were sitting on top of one of the deepest open questions in the subject. I owe that lecturer an apology for all the sighing.

---

## What a Tautology Is

A **tautology** is a formula that comes out **true** under every possible assignment of its variables. It doesn't matter what you plug in. It's always true.

The simplest one is:

$$x \vee \neg x$$

"$x$ is true, or $x$ is false." If $x$ is true, the left side is true. If $x$ is false, the right side is true. There's no way to make it false. In everyday language, "it's raining or it isn't" is a tautology. It's always true, and it tells you nothing about the weather.

Before going further I need two new symbols. You'll see them in the figure.

:::info New Symbols: $\to$ and $\leftrightarrow$
**$x \to y$** means "if $x$ then $y$". It is only false in one situation: when $x$ is true and $y$ is false. If $x$ is false, the statement is counted as true no matter what $y$ is. (This felt like cheating to me at first. Think of a promise: "if it rains, I'll bring an umbrella". If it doesn't rain, I haven't broken my promise, whatever I do.)

**$x \leftrightarrow y$** means "$x$ and $y$ have the same truth value": both true, or both false.
:::

> **TAUTOLOGY:** given a Boolean formula, is it true under every possible assignment?

A few that surprised me:

- **Formula 2** is De Morgan's law. "Not (x and y)" means the same as "not x, or not y". It's true on every row.
- **Formula 3** says "if $x$ implies $y$, then $y$ implies $x$". It sounds reasonable, and people make this mistake all the time in arguments. "If it's a cat, it has four legs" does not mean "if it has four legs, it's a cat". The truth table finds the row that breaks it immediately.
- **Formula 5** is a tautology even though it looks like it says something. Look closely: it contains "$\neg y$" in one half and "$y$" in the other, so one of the two halves always wins.

Now notice the asymmetry in the status line:

- When a formula is **not** a tautology, one false row is enough to prove it. You can check that one row in a moment.
- When a formula **is** a tautology, you had to look at **every** row. With $n$ variables, that's $2^n$ rows.

That's exactly the shape of $co\mathcal{NP}$ from Post 25. Short proofs for **no**. No known short proofs for **yes**.

---

## TAUTOLOGY Is SAT, Looked at Upside Down

Here's the connection that made this click for me.

A formula $\phi$ is a tautology if it's true on every row. Now look at its negation, $\neg \phi$. On every row where $\phi$ is true, $\neg \phi$ is false. So:

$$\phi \text{ is a tautology} \iff \neg \phi \text{ is unsatisfiable}$$

"Always true" and "never true" are mirror images. If you could decide one quickly, you could decide the other by sticking a NOT in front.

So TAUTOLOGY and UNSAT from Post 25 are essentially the same problem. And UNSAT is the complement of SAT. So TAUTOLOGY is in $co\mathcal{NP}$.

## The Hardest Problems in coNP

In Phase 2, we called a problem **NP-Complete** if it's in $\mathcal{NP}$ and every problem in $\mathcal{NP}$ reduces to it. The same idea works for $co\mathcal{NP}$:

:::info coNP-Complete
A problem is **coNP-Complete** if it's in $co\mathcal{NP}$, and every problem in $co\mathcal{NP}$ reduces to it in polynomial time.
:::

**TAUTOLOGY is coNP-Complete.** And the proof is short, because Cook-Levin from Post 12 already did all the work. Watch.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="step1" label="1. Start with any coNP problem" default>

Take any problem $L$ in $co\mathcal{NP}$. By definition, its complement $\overline{L}$ is in $\mathcal{NP}$.

(The bar over $L$ means "complement" here, the same idea as the complement graph $\overline{G}$ from Post 16: flip everything. For a problem, that means flipping every yes and no.)

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="step2" label="2. Use Cook-Levin">

Cook-Levin says every problem in $\mathcal{NP}$ reduces to SAT. So there's a fast conversion $f$ that turns any input $x$ into a formula $f(x)$, with:

$$x \in \overline{L} \iff f(x) \text{ is satisfiable}$$

Flipping both sides:

$$x \in L \iff f(x) \text{ is unsatisfiable}$$

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="step3" label="3. Add a NOT">

A formula is unsatisfiable exactly when its negation is a tautology. So:

$$x \in L \iff \neg f(x) \text{ is a tautology}$$

The conversion is: compute $f(x)$, put a NOT in front. Still polynomial. So $L$ reduces to TAUTOLOGY.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Since $L$ was any problem in $co\mathcal{NP}$, every one of them reduces to TAUTOLOGY. It's coNP-Complete.

And the flip side, which ties back to Post 25: if TAUTOLOGY turned out to be in $\mathcal{NP}$, meaning every tautology had a short certificate, then every $co\mathcal{NP}$ problem would be in $\mathcal{NP}$, and the two classes would be equal.

## A Funny Twist: Which Shape Is Easy?

Remember CNF from Post 13: an AND of ORs, like $(x \vee y) \wedge (\neg x \vee z)$. SAT for CNF formulas is NP-Complete.

There's an opposite shape called **DNF**: an OR of ANDs, like $(x \wedge y) \vee (\neg x \wedge z)$. And the difficulty flips with the shape:

| | CNF (AND of ORs) | DNF (OR of ANDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Is it **satisfiable**? | NP-Complete | Easy: just check if any one AND-group doesn't contain both a variable and its negation |
| Is it a **tautology**? | Easy: every OR-group must contain some variable and its negation | coNP-Complete |

Easy and hard swap places when you swap the shape and the question. Each easy case has a quick, local check. Each hard case requires reasoning about all the groups at once. Formula 6 in the figure above is a DNF, and it's exactly the hard kind of tautology question.

## Does Every True Statement Have a Short Proof?

This is the part I found most interesting, and it goes beyond truth tables.

In maths, you don't prove things by checking every case. You write a **proof**: a sequence of steps, each following from earlier ones by fixed rules. For tautologies, logicians have designed many **proof systems**, sets of rules for deriving tautologies step by step.

Truth tables are one, terrible, proof system: the proof is the whole table, $2^n$ rows long. The question is whether some cleverer system could always give **short** proofs.

In 1979, Stephen Cook (the Cook of Cook-Levin) and Robert Reckhow made this precise and proved:

> $\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}$ **if and only if** there is a proof system in which every tautology has a proof of polynomial length.

So the question "is $\mathcal{NP}$ equal to $co\mathcal{NP}$?" is the same as "does every logical truth have a short proof, in some system?". That reframing started a whole research area called **proof complexity**. Researchers take specific proof systems, one at a time, and try to prove that some tautologies need very long proofs in them.

The most famous early success involves the **pigeonhole principle**: if you put $n + 1$ pigeons into $n$ holes, some hole has two pigeons. Obviously true. You can write it as a tautology. In 1985, Armin Haken proved that in a widely used proof system called **resolution**, any proof of the pigeonhole tautology needs exponentially many steps.

That's a real lower bound, but only for one proof system. To show $\mathcal{NP} \neq co\mathcal{NP}$, you'd need to do it for **every** possible proof system. That's the same kind of "rule out everything" problem that makes P vs NP so hard, and Phase 4 is all about why.

Resolution is not just a theoretical toy, by the way. It's closely related to how modern SAT solvers work. When a solver reports that a formula is unsatisfiable, its run can be turned into a resolution proof. So Haken's result also says something practical: there are small-looking formulas that some of the most common solver techniques will take exponential time to refute.

## Where We Stand

:::note What We Have Established
A **tautology** is a formula true under every assignment. $\phi$ is a tautology exactly when $\neg \phi$ is unsatisfiable.

**TAUTOLOGY is coNP-Complete**: Cook-Levin turns any $co\mathcal{NP}$ problem into "is this formula unsatisfiable?", and a NOT turns that into "is this a tautology?".

$\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}$ exactly when some proof system gives every tautology a polynomial-length proof (Cook and Reckhow, 1979). Specific systems like resolution are known to need exponential-length proofs for some tautologies, such as the pigeonhole principle (Haken, 1985).
:::
