The Class coNP

Welcome to Phase 3.

For twenty-four posts, the whole world has been two classes: P\mathcal{P}P and NP\mathcal{NP}NP. That's the question in the title of this whole project, so it made sense to focus on them. But I always had a slight suspicion that I was looking at two animals in a much bigger zoo.

I was right. Complexity theorists have defined hundreds of classes. Scott Aaronson, the researcher I quoted at the very top of Post 1, has run an online catalogue for years called the Complexity Zoo that lists over five hundred of them. I am not going to cover five hundred. This phase covers the handful that actually matter for understanding P vs NP.

And the first one came straight out of the last post.

The Question From Minesweeper​

At the end of Post 24, there were two questions about a Minesweeper board:

  • Is this board consistent? To convince you, I show you one mine layout that fits. You check it quickly.
  • Is this square definitely safe? To convince you, showing one layout where it's safe isn't enough. I have to somehow convince you that no layout puts a mine there.

The first one has a short proof when the answer is yes. The second one has a short proof when the answer is no: if the square is not safe, I just show you one valid layout with a mine on it.

That flip, short proofs for yes versus short proofs for no, is what this post is about.

A Reminder: What NP Means​

From Post 8: a problem is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP if, whenever the answer is yes, there is a short certificate that proves it, and that certificate can be checked in polynomial time.

When the answer is no, NP\mathcal{NP}NP promises nothing at all.

Take SAT from Post 13. If a formula is satisfiable, the certificate is a satisfying assignment. You plug it in and check every clause. Fast.

If a formula is not satisfiable, what would you hand me as proof? One assignment that fails proves nothing, because another assignment might work. You'd have to show me that all 2n2^n2n assignments fail. Nobody knows a short way to do that in general.

Flipping the Question​

Now consider the opposite problem:

UNSAT: given a Boolean formula, is it unsatisfiable? That is, does every possible assignment make it false?

UNSAT is SAT with the yes and no swapped. A formula is a yes-instance of UNSAT exactly when it's a no-instance of SAT.

The opposite of a problem like this is called its complement. And now the short proof has moved to the other side:

  • When the answer to UNSAT is no (the formula can be satisfied), there's a short proof: a satisfying assignment.
  • When the answer is yes (the formula can never be satisfied), no short proof is known.
The Class coNP

A problem is in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP if its complement is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP.

Equivalently: whenever the answer is no, there's a short certificate that proves it, checkable in polynomial time.

The "co" stands for "complement". It's pronounced "co-N-P".

So UNSAT is in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP. So is "is this Minesweeper square safe?". So is "does this graph have no Hamiltonian cycle?".

A useful way I found to think about it:

Short proof exists for...Sounds like...
NP\mathcal{NP}NPyes answers"there exists a solution"
coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNPno answers"for every possibility, it works out"

NP\mathcal{NP}NP questions are "is there some way?". coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP questions are "does it hold every way?".

Where coNP Sits​

Here's the little map of the zoo I'm going to use throughout this phase. A line from a lower class to a higher one means "proven to be inside". Click a class to read what it means and what's known to be inside it. Right now, coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP is selected.

I want to be careful about what the picture says. A missing line means "nobody knows", not "they're different". For example, there's no line between NP\mathcal{NP}NP and coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, because nobody knows whether one contains the other.

What we do know:

P\mathcal{P}P is inside coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP. If you can solve a problem quickly, you can solve its complement quickly too. Just run the same algorithm and flip the answer. So every problem in P\mathcal{P}P has its complement in P\mathcal{P}P, which is inside NP\mathcal{NP}NP. That puts the original problem in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP.

So P\mathcal{P}P sits inside both: P⊆NP∩coNP\mathcal{P} \subseteq \mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}P⊆NP∩coNP.

New Symbol: ∩\cap∩

Read it as "intersect", or just "and". NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP is the set of problems that are in NP\mathcal{NP}NP and in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP at the same time: problems where both yes and no answers have short proofs.

Does NP Equal coNP?​

This is an open question too, and it's tied directly to P vs NP.

If P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP, then NP=coNP\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP. Here's why. If every NP\mathcal{NP}NP problem were in P\mathcal{P}P, then every coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP problem would have its complement in P\mathcal{P}P, and since P\mathcal{P}P is closed under flipping answers, the problem itself would be in P\mathcal{P}P too. So both classes would just be P\mathcal{P}P.

Turn that around and you get something interesting:

NP≠coNP  ⟹  P≠NP\mathcal{NP} \neq co\mathcal{NP} \implies \mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}NP=coNP⟹P=NP

New Symbol:   ⟹  \implies⟹

Read it as "implies". "A  ⟹  BA \implies BA⟹B" means "if AAA is true, then BBB is true". It's a one-way arrow, unlike   ⟺  \iff⟺ from Post 16.

So proving NP≠coNP\mathcal{NP} \neq co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP would be even stronger than proving P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP. It would solve the Millennium Prize problem as a side effect. Most researchers believe NP≠coNP\mathcal{NP} \neq co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP, for the same kind of reason they believe P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP: nobody has ever found a short way to prove a formula is unsatisfiable in general, despite enormous effort.

And there's one more link. If any NP-Complete problem, like SAT, turned out to also be in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, then all of NP\mathcal{NP}NP would collapse into coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, and the two would be equal. That's why finding a problem in NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP is a strong hint that it's not NP-Complete.

Prime Numbers and Factoring: A Correction I Had to Make​

When I first planned this post, my notes said: "coNP example: prime factorisation". When I actually looked into it, I realised that's not quite right, and the true story is better. So here it is properly.

  • Is N composite?
  • Is N prime?
  • Factoring

Composite means "not prime": the number can be split into smaller whole-number factors. For example, 91=7×1391 = 7 \times 1391=7×13.

If NNN is composite, the certificate is easy: give me one factor. I divide and check. So "is NNN composite?" is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP.

Its complement is "is NNN prime?". So primality is in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP: if a number is not prime, a factor proves it.

For a while, that's where the story seemed to stop. How would you give a short proof that a number is prime?

In 1975, Vaughan Pratt showed that you can. There's a clever kind of certificate, based on a number theory fact about prime numbers, that can be checked quickly. So primality is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP too. It's in NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP.

Then in 2002, Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena, a professor and two students at IIT Kanpur, found a polynomial-time algorithm for primality. It's called the AKS test. Primality is in P\mathcal{P}P, which Post 7 already mentioned.

That's the full journey: coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, then NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP, then P\mathcal{P}P.

Factoring is different from primality. Knowing that NNN is not prime doesn't tell you its factors.

As a yes/no question: "does NNN have a factor smaller than kkk?"

  • Yes certificate: the factor. Check it divides NNN and is smaller than kkk.
  • No certificate: the complete prime factorisation of NNN. Multiply the primes together to check they make NNN, check each one is prime (fast, thanks to AKS), and see that none of them is smaller than kkk.

Both answers have short certificates. Factoring is in NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP.

Nobody knows a polynomial-time algorithm for it, so it's not known to be in P\mathcal{P}P. But because it sits in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP as well, it is very unlikely to be NP-Complete. That's exactly why the diagram in Post 10 put factoring in its own special spot, inside NP\mathcal{NP}NP but outside the NP-Complete region.

This matters a lot for Phase 5. RSA encryption relies on factoring being hard, but factoring is probably not one of the hardest problems in NP\mathcal{NP}NP.

Why I Care About This Class​

When I first met coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, it felt like bookkeeping. Flip the answers, add "co" to the name, done.

What changed my mind was realising that "is there a short proof of no?" is one of the most practical questions in the world. A chip designer wants to know that a circuit can never produce a wrong output. A software engineer wants to know that a program can never crash. Someone verifying a security protocol wants to know that no attack works. Those are all "for every possibility" questions. They live in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP or above.

Proving something can never happen is harder than showing that it can. That asymmetry, which we'll keep running into, has a precise home now.

Where We Stand​

What We Have Established

coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP is the class of problems whose complement is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP. When the answer is no, there's a short certificate that can be checked quickly. UNSAT, "is this square safe?" and "is this number prime?" are all in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP.

P⊆NP∩coNP\mathcal{P} \subseteq \mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}P⊆NP∩coNP. Whether NP=coNP\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP is unknown. If P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP then NP=coNP\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP, so proving they're different would also prove P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP.

Primality went from coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP to NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP (Pratt, 1975) to P\mathcal{P}P (AKS, 2002). Factoring is in NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP and not known to be in P\mathcal{P}P, which is why it is believed not to be NP-Complete.

Up Next​

NP\mathcal{NP}NP has its hardest problems: the NP-Complete ones, with SAT at the top. Does coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP have hardest problems too?

It does. Post 26 is about tautologies: formulas that are true no matter what you plug in. Deciding whether a formula is a tautology is the coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP version of SAT, and it's connected to a very old question about how long a proof has to be.
