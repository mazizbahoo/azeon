Tautologies and coNP-Completeness

In my first-year logic lectures, we spent a whole week drawing truth tables. Rows and rows of T and F, for formulas with names like "De Morgan's law" and "the law of the excluded middle". At the time I thought it was the most boring thing I'd ever been asked to do.

Then I started this phase and found out that those boring tables were sitting on top of one of the deepest open questions in the subject. I owe that lecturer an apology for all the sighing.

What a Tautology Is​

A tautology is a formula that comes out true under every possible assignment of its variables. It doesn't matter what you plug in. It's always true.

The simplest one is:

x∨¬xx \vee \neg xx∨¬x

"xxx is true, or xxx is false." If xxx is true, the left side is true. If xxx is false, the right side is true. There's no way to make it false. In everyday language, "it's raining or it isn't" is a tautology. It's always true, and it tells you nothing about the weather.

Before going further I need two new symbols. You'll see them in the figure.

New Symbols: →\to→ and ↔\leftrightarrow↔

x→yx \to yx→y means "if xxx then yyy". It is only false in one situation: when xxx is true and yyy is false. If xxx is false, the statement is counted as true no matter what yyy is. (This felt like cheating to me at first. Think of a promise: "if it rains, I'll bring an umbrella". If it doesn't rain, I haven't broken my promise, whatever I do.)

x↔yx \leftrightarrow yx↔y means "xxx and yyy have the same truth value": both true, or both false.

TAUTOLOGY: given a Boolean formula, is it true under every possible assignment?

Check Some Yourself​

Here are six formulas. For each one, the figure shows the full truth table: every possible assignment, one per row, and what the formula comes out as.

A few that surprised me:

  • Formula 2 is De Morgan's law. "Not (x and y)" means the same as "not x, or not y". It's true on every row.
  • Formula 3 says "if xxx implies yyy, then yyy implies xxx". It sounds reasonable, and people make this mistake all the time in arguments. "If it's a cat, it has four legs" does not mean "if it has four legs, it's a cat". The truth table finds the row that breaks it immediately.
  • Formula 5 is a tautology even though it looks like it says something. Look closely: it contains "¬y\neg y¬y" in one half and "yyy" in the other, so one of the two halves always wins.

Now notice the asymmetry in the status line:

  • When a formula is not a tautology, one false row is enough to prove it. You can check that one row in a moment.
  • When a formula is a tautology, you had to look at every row. With nnn variables, that's 2n2^n2n rows.

That's exactly the shape of coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP from Post 25. Short proofs for no. No known short proofs for yes.

TAUTOLOGY Is SAT, Looked at Upside Down​

Here's the connection that made this click for me.

A formula ϕ\phiϕ is a tautology if it's true on every row. Now look at its negation, ¬ϕ\neg \phi¬ϕ. On every row where ϕ\phiϕ is true, ¬ϕ\neg \phi¬ϕ is false. So:

ϕ is a tautology  ⟺  ¬ϕ is unsatisfiable\phi \text{ is a tautology} \iff \neg \phi \text{ is unsatisfiable}ϕ is a tautology⟺¬ϕ is unsatisfiable

"Always true" and "never true" are mirror images. If you could decide one quickly, you could decide the other by sticking a NOT in front.

So TAUTOLOGY and UNSAT from Post 25 are essentially the same problem. And UNSAT is the complement of SAT. So TAUTOLOGY is in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP.

The Hardest Problems in coNP​

In Phase 2, we called a problem NP-Complete if it's in NP\mathcal{NP}NP and every problem in NP\mathcal{NP}NP reduces to it. The same idea works for coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP:

coNP-Complete

A problem is coNP-Complete if it's in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, and every problem in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP reduces to it in polynomial time.

TAUTOLOGY is coNP-Complete. And the proof is short, because Cook-Levin from Post 12 already did all the work. Watch.

  • 1. Start with any coNP problem
  • 2. Use Cook-Levin
  • 3. Add a NOT

Take any problem LLL in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP. By definition, its complement L‾\overline{L}L is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP.

(The bar over LLL means "complement" here, the same idea as the complement graph G‾\overline{G}G from Post 16: flip everything. For a problem, that means flipping every yes and no.)

Cook-Levin says every problem in NP\mathcal{NP}NP reduces to SAT. So there's a fast conversion fff that turns any input xxx into a formula f(x)f(x)f(x), with:

x∈L‾  ⟺  f(x) is satisfiablex \in \overline{L} \iff f(x) \text{ is satisfiable}x∈L⟺f(x) is satisfiable

Flipping both sides:

x∈L  ⟺  f(x) is unsatisfiablex \in L \iff f(x) \text{ is unsatisfiable}x∈L⟺f(x) is unsatisfiable

A formula is unsatisfiable exactly when its negation is a tautology. So:

x∈L  ⟺  ¬f(x) is a tautologyx \in L \iff \neg f(x) \text{ is a tautology}x∈L⟺¬f(x) is a tautology

The conversion is: compute f(x)f(x)f(x), put a NOT in front. Still polynomial. So LLL reduces to TAUTOLOGY.

Since LLL was any problem in coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP, every one of them reduces to TAUTOLOGY. It's coNP-Complete.

And the flip side, which ties back to Post 25: if TAUTOLOGY turned out to be in NP\mathcal{NP}NP, meaning every tautology had a short certificate, then every coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP problem would be in NP\mathcal{NP}NP, and the two classes would be equal.

A Funny Twist: Which Shape Is Easy?​

Remember CNF from Post 13: an AND of ORs, like (x∨y)∧(¬x∨z)(x \vee y) \wedge (\neg x \vee z)(x∨y)∧(¬x∨z). SAT for CNF formulas is NP-Complete.

There's an opposite shape called DNF: an OR of ANDs, like (x∧y)∨(¬x∧z)(x \wedge y) \vee (\neg x \wedge z)(x∧y)∨(¬x∧z). And the difficulty flips with the shape:

CNF (AND of ORs)DNF (OR of ANDs)
Is it satisfiable?NP-CompleteEasy: just check if any one AND-group doesn't contain both a variable and its negation
Is it a tautology?Easy: every OR-group must contain some variable and its negationcoNP-Complete

Easy and hard swap places when you swap the shape and the question. Each easy case has a quick, local check. Each hard case requires reasoning about all the groups at once. Formula 6 in the figure above is a DNF, and it's exactly the hard kind of tautology question.

Does Every True Statement Have a Short Proof?​

This is the part I found most interesting, and it goes beyond truth tables.

In maths, you don't prove things by checking every case. You write a proof: a sequence of steps, each following from earlier ones by fixed rules. For tautologies, logicians have designed many proof systems, sets of rules for deriving tautologies step by step.

Truth tables are one, terrible, proof system: the proof is the whole table, 2n2^n2n rows long. The question is whether some cleverer system could always give short proofs.

In 1979, Stephen Cook (the Cook of Cook-Levin) and Robert Reckhow made this precise and proved:

NP=coNP\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP if and only if there is a proof system in which every tautology has a proof of polynomial length.

So the question "is NP\mathcal{NP}NP equal to coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP?" is the same as "does every logical truth have a short proof, in some system?". That reframing started a whole research area called proof complexity. Researchers take specific proof systems, one at a time, and try to prove that some tautologies need very long proofs in them.

The most famous early success involves the pigeonhole principle: if you put n+1n + 1n+1 pigeons into nnn holes, some hole has two pigeons. Obviously true. You can write it as a tautology. In 1985, Armin Haken proved that in a widely used proof system called resolution, any proof of the pigeonhole tautology needs exponentially many steps.

That's a real lower bound, but only for one proof system. To show NP≠coNP\mathcal{NP} \neq co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP, you'd need to do it for every possible proof system. That's the same kind of "rule out everything" problem that makes P vs NP so hard, and Phase 4 is all about why.

Resolution is not just a theoretical toy, by the way. It's closely related to how modern SAT solvers work. When a solver reports that a formula is unsatisfiable, its run can be turned into a resolution proof. So Haken's result also says something practical: there are small-looking formulas that some of the most common solver techniques will take exponential time to refute.

Where We Stand​

What We Have Established

A tautology is a formula true under every assignment. ϕ\phiϕ is a tautology exactly when ¬ϕ\neg \phi¬ϕ is unsatisfiable.

TAUTOLOGY is coNP-Complete: Cook-Levin turns any coNPco\mathcal{NP}coNP problem into "is this formula unsatisfiable?", and a NOT turns that into "is this a tautology?".

NP=coNP\mathcal{NP} = co\mathcal{NP}NP=coNP exactly when some proof system gives every tautology a polynomial-length proof (Cook and Reckhow, 1979). Specific systems like resolution are known to need exponential-length proofs for some tautologies, such as the pigeonhole principle (Haken, 1985).

Up Next​

I've been using the words "NP-Hard" and "NP-Complete" as if they were almost the same thing. They aren't, and the difference matters.

Post 27 pulls them apart. NP-Hard problems don't even have to be in NP\mathcal{NP}NP. Some of them can't be solved by any computer at all, ever. And yet they're still "NP-Hard", because the label only says how hard a problem is from below, not how hard it is from above.
