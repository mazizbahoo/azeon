Quantum Computing and BQP

If you've ever watched a video about quantum computers, you've probably heard this sentence: "a quantum computer tries every possible answer at the same time."

I believed that sentence for a long time. It's the reason I assumed quantum computers would make P vs NP irrelevant. If you can try every answer at once, then 2n2^n2n possibilities is no problem, right?

That sentence is wrong. Not slightly wrong, wrong in the way that matters most. Getting it right took me a few weeks of reading, mostly Scott Aaronson's writing and lecture notes, and this post and the next two are what came out of it. I'm going to try to explain it without any physics beyond what's absolutely necessary.

Bits and Qubits​

A normal bit is 0 or 1. Always one, never both.

A qubit is described by two numbers called amplitudes, one attached to 0 and one attached to 1. You can think of them as "how much 0" and "how much 1" the qubit currently has. We write the state as:

α ∣0⟩+β ∣1⟩\alpha \, |0\rangle + \beta \, |1\rangleα∣0⟩+β∣1⟩

New Symbols: ∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩, α\alphaα, β\betaβ

∣0⟩|0\rangle∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩|1\rangle∣1⟩ are just names for "the 0 state" and "the 1 state". The odd brackets are physics notation, and you can read them as labels.

α\alphaα (alpha) and β\betaβ (beta) are the amplitudes. In real quantum computers they can be complex numbers, but everything important in this post works with ordinary positive and negative numbers.

When you measure a qubit, you don't see the amplitudes. You see a plain 0 or a plain 1, and the qubit becomes that value. The probability of seeing 0 is α2\alpha^2α2, and the probability of seeing 1 is β2\beta^2β2. Those two always add up to 1.

So far this sounds like a coin that's been flipped but not looked at. 50% heads, 50% tails. And if that's all it was, quantum computers would just be randomized computers from Post 31.

The difference is the minus sign.

Amplitudes Can Cancel​

Probabilities are never negative. If two different paths lead to the same outcome, their probabilities add up, and the outcome gets more likely.

Amplitudes can be negative. If two paths lead to the same outcome, one with amplitude +0.5+0.5+0.5 and the other with −0.5-0.5−0.5, they add up to zero. The outcome becomes impossible, even though each path on its own would have led there.

That's called interference. It's the same thing that happens with waves on water: two crests meeting make a bigger wave, a crest meeting a trough flattens out.

Here's a single qubit you can play with. It starts as a definite 0. There are three operations you can apply:

  • H (Hadamard) spreads the qubit evenly between 0 and 1, but with a minus sign on the 1 side when it starts from 1.
  • X swaps 0 and 1, like a NOT gate.
  • Z flips the sign of the 1 amplitude.

Try the three presets:

  1. H: from 0, you get amplitudes +0.707+0.707+0.707 and +0.707+0.707+0.707. Each squares to 0.5. A fair 50/50 coin.
  2. H H: you'd expect two coin flips to still be a 50/50 coin. Instead, you're back to a definite 0, 100%. The two paths leading to 1 had opposite signs and cancelled out. The paths leading to 0 had the same sign and reinforced.
  3. H Z H: the same as before, but the Z in the middle flips one sign. Now the cancellation happens on the other side, and you get a definite 1.

That second one is the whole of quantum computing in miniature. Applying a "randomizing" step twice produced certainty, because the possibilities interfered. No coin could ever do that.

Many Qubits​

With nnn qubits, there's an amplitude for every combination of nnn bits. That's 2n2^n2n amplitudes. Ten qubits have 1,024 amplitudes. Three hundred qubits have more amplitudes than there are atoms in the observable universe.

This is where the "tries every answer" story comes from. A quantum computer can, in a sense, put weight on all 2n2^n2n possibilities at once.

But here's the catch: you only get to measure once, and when you do, you see one random outcome, with probability given by the squared amplitudes. If you just spread the amplitudes evenly and measure, you get a random answer. That's no better than guessing.

The real job of a quantum algorithm

A quantum algorithm has to arrange the operations so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel each other out, and the amplitudes of right answers reinforce. Then, when you measure, you're likely to see a right answer.

That only works for problems with special structure that lets the cancellations line up. For most problems, nobody knows how to do it.

That's the honest version. Not "tries every answer". It's "choreographs interference so wrong answers cancel".

The Class BQP​

The Class BQP

A yes/no problem is in BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP (Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time) if a quantum computer can solve it using a polynomial number of operations, giving the right answer with probability at least 23\frac{2}{3}32​ on every input.

It's BPP\mathcal{BPP}BPP from Post 31, with qubits instead of coins. The same "repeat and vote" amplification works, so 23\frac{2}{3}32​ can be pushed as close to 1 as you want. The class was defined formally by Ethan Bernstein and Umesh Vazirani in 1993.

Where does it sit?

BPP⊆BQP\mathcal{BPP} \subseteq \mathcal{BQP}BPP⊆BQP. A quantum computer can produce fair coin flips (one H and a measurement), so it can do anything a randomized computer can.

BQP⊆PSPACE\mathcal{BQP} \subseteq \mathcal{PSPACE}BQP⊆PSPACE. This one surprised me. It means a normal computer can simulate a quantum computer, with only polynomial memory. The trick is to compute the amplitude of each final outcome by adding up the contributions of all the paths that lead to it, one path at a time, reusing memory just like in Post 28. It takes exponential time, but not exponential memory.

So quantum computers can't solve anything that's impossible for normal computers. The Halting Problem stays unsolvable. The question is only about speed.

And the big one: how does BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP relate to NP\mathcal{NP}NP? Nobody knows. There are problems in BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP, like factoring, that aren't known to be in BPP\mathcal{BPP}BPP. And there's strong evidence, which I'll explain in Post 34, that NP\mathcal{NP}NP-Complete problems are not in BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP.

Where the Speedups Come From​

Some history, so you can see this didn't come from nowhere.

  • 1982: Richard Feynman argued that simulating quantum physics on normal computers seems to need exponential resources, and suggested building computers out of quantum systems instead.
  • 1985: David Deutsch described a universal quantum computer, the quantum version of Turing's machine from Post 3.
  • 1994: Daniel Simon found a problem, in a special "black box" setting, where quantum computers are exponentially faster than any randomized computer. His idea directly inspired the next one.
  • 1994: Peter Shor found a quantum algorithm for factoring numbers in polynomial time. That's Post 33, and it's the reason governments care.

Do Real Quantum Computers Exist?​

They do, but they're small and noisy. Today's machines have hundreds or a few thousand physical qubits, and those qubits make errors constantly. Fixing errors needs many physical qubits for every reliable "logical" qubit.

In 2019, Google reported that its 53-qubit Sycamore processor had performed a specific sampling task in about 200 seconds that, by its estimate, would take a classical supercomputer thousands of years. IBM disputed that estimate, and later work found much faster classical methods for that particular task. That back-and-forth is pretty typical of the field right now: every claimed advantage gets tested hard, which is exactly how it should be.

The tasks in these experiments were chosen because they suit quantum hardware, not because anyone needed the answers. They are demonstrations, on the road to machines big enough to run something like Shor's algorithm.

Where We Stand​

What We Have Established

A qubit is described by amplitudes, which can be negative. Measuring gives 0 or 1 with probability equal to the squared amplitude. Because amplitudes can cancel, quantum computers can make wrong answers interfere away. That's the real source of their power, not "trying every answer at once".

BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP is the class of problems a quantum computer solves in polynomial time with error at most 13\frac{1}{3}31​. BPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE\mathcal{BPP} \subseteq \mathcal{BQP} \subseteq \mathcal{PSPACE}BPP⊆BQP⊆PSPACE, so quantum computers can't solve anything a normal computer can't solve eventually. They may be exponentially faster on specially structured problems. How BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP relates to NP\mathcal{NP}NP is unknown.
