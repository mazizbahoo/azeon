Approximation Algorithms

The first time I read the phrase "this algorithm always finds a solution within twice the optimum", I didn't believe it. How can you promise you're close to the best answer if you don't know what the best answer is? It felt like saying "I'm within ten minutes of your house" without knowing where your house is.

It turns out you can, and the trick is beautiful: you find something you do know about the best answer, a bound that the optimum can't beat, and compare against that instead. This post is about the idea in general. The next three posts are specific, famous examples.

What an Approximation Guarantee Says​

Take a minimisation problem, like finding the smallest vertex cover. Call the size of the best possible answer OPT\text{OPT}OPT.

Approximation Ratio

An algorithm is a ccc-approximation if, on every input, it runs in polynomial time and returns an answer of cost at most c×OPTc \times \text{OPT}c×OPT.

For a maximisation problem, it's the other way round: the answer is always at least OPT/c\text{OPT} / cOPT/c, or equivalently at least a fraction 1/c1/c1/c of the best.

So a 2-approximation for vertex cover always finds a cover at most twice as big as the smallest one. If the best cover has 50 nodes, you're guaranteed at most 100.

Three things about this definition matter:

  • It holds on every input, including the worst one. It's a promise, not an average.
  • It runs in polynomial time. That's the whole point.
  • It doesn't tell you OPT\text{OPT}OPT. It only tells you how far away you could possibly be.

The Trick: Compare Against a Bound​

How do you prove "at most twice the optimum" without knowing the optimum? You find a quantity that you can compute, and that's guaranteed to be at most OPT\text{OPT}OPT (for minimisation). Then you show your answer is at most twice that.

your answer≤2×bound≤2×OPT\text{your answer} \leq 2 \times \text{bound} \leq 2 \times \text{OPT}your answer≤2×bound≤2×OPT

Every approximation proof I've read has this shape. The creativity is all in finding the right bound. For vertex cover, the bound is a matching (Post 65). For TSP, it's a spanning tree (Post 66). For many problems, it's a relaxation that allows fractions, the idea from Post 62.

A Lovely Example: Just Guess Randomly​

Here's the simplest approximation algorithm I know. It's for MAX-3SAT: given a 3-SAT formula, satisfy as many clauses as possible (even if you can't satisfy them all).

The algorithm: flip a coin for every variable.

Why is that any good? Take a clause with three different variables, like (x1∨¬x4∨x7)(x_1 \vee \neg x_4 \vee x_7)(x1​∨¬x4​∨x7​). It's only false if all three literals are false. Each is false with probability 12\frac{1}{2}21​, independently, so the clause is false with probability 18\frac{1}{8}81​. So it's true with probability 78\frac{7}{8}87​.

That's true of every clause. So on average, a random assignment satisfies 78\frac{7}{8}87​ of all the clauses. And since no assignment can satisfy more than all of them, that's at least 78\frac{7}{8}87​ of the optimum.

Try it on a formula with 40 clauses:

Random assignments hover around 35, sometimes a bit more, sometimes a bit less. The average settles near 35 as you add more runs.

No luck needed shows that you don't even need luck. Go through the variables one at a time. For each one, work out the expected number of satisfied clauses if you set it true (with the rest still random), and the same if you set it false. Pick whichever is higher. The expected count never drops below its starting value of 78×40=35\frac{7}{8} \times 40 = 3587​×40=35, and once every variable is fixed, nothing is random any more, so you have an assignment that satisfies at least 35 clauses, guaranteed. This trick is called the method of conditional expectations, and it turns many randomized algorithms into deterministic ones.

And you can't do better​

Here's the astonishing part. In 2001, Johan Håstad proved that, unless P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP, no polynomial-time algorithm can guarantee satisfying more than 78+ε\frac{7}{8} + \varepsilon87​+ε of the optimum, for any ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, on formulas where every clause has exactly three literals.

New Symbol: ε\varepsilonε

The Greek letter epsilon, ε\varepsilonε, is what mathematicians use for "some small positive number, as small as you like". "78+ε\frac{7}{8} + \varepsilon87​+ε" means "anything even slightly better than 78\frac{7}{8}87​".

So flipping coins is optimal. The dumbest possible algorithm achieves the best guarantee that any efficient algorithm can. When I read that, I had to put my phone down for a minute.

The Spectrum: From Great to Impossible​

Different NP-Hard problems allow very different guarantees, even though they're all equally hard to solve exactly (they all reduce to each other). This was one of the biggest surprises for me in this whole phase.

For set cover (the crew-pairing problem from Post 55, without the "exactly once" rule), a simple greedy rule works: keep choosing the set that covers the most still-uncovered items for its cost. This was analysed in the 1970s by David Johnson, László Lovász and Vašek Chvátal, and is guaranteed to be within about ln⁡n\ln nlnn of optimal, where nnn is the number of items.

New Symbol: ln⁡\lnln

ln⁡n\ln nlnn is the natural logarithm: a logarithm with a special base, about 2.718. It grows very slowly. ln⁡1,000,000\ln 1{,}000{,}000ln1,000,000 is only about 14.

And in 2014, Irit Dinur and David Steurer proved that doing noticeably better than ln⁡n\ln nlnn is NP-Hard. Greedy is essentially the best possible.

Take TSP where distances can be anything, not following any geometric rules.

Recall the reduction from Post 20: real roads get distance 1, missing roads get distance 2. Change the 2 to something huge, like 1000×n1000 \times n1000×n. If the graph has a Hamiltonian cycle, the best tour costs nnn. If not, every tour uses at least one missing road, so it costs more than 1000×n1000 \times n1000×n.

Now suppose some algorithm always got within a factor of 1000 of optimal. On a graph with a Hamiltonian cycle, it would return a tour costing at most 1000×n1000 \times n1000×n, which means a tour using only real roads. So it would decide Hamiltonian Cycle, which is NP-Complete.

The same argument works for any factor, however big. So unless P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP, general TSP can't be approximated within any factor that can be computed in polynomial time. Sartaj Sahni and Teofilo Gonzalez proved this in 1976.

Where do these "you can't do better" results come from? Mostly from the PCP theorem in Post 42. It turned out that "proofs you can check by reading a few random bits" are exactly the tool needed to show that even approximately solving many problems is NP-Hard. That was one of the great surprises of 1990s complexity theory.

What We Have Established

A ccc-approximation runs in polynomial time and always returns an answer within a factor ccc of optimal, proved by comparing the answer to a computable bound on OPT\text{OPT}OPT.

For MAX-3SAT, a random assignment satisfies 78\frac{7}{8}87​ of the clauses on average, and the method of conditional expectations makes this a guarantee. Håstad (2001) proved that beating 78\frac{7}{8}87​ is NP-Hard, so this is optimal.

Approximability varies widely: knapsack can be approximated arbitrarily well, vertex cover within 2, set cover within about ln⁡n\ln nlnn (and no better, Dinur and Steurer 2014), clique hardly at all, and general TSP not at all unless P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP (Sahni and Gonzalez, 1976). Most of these limits come from the PCP theorem.
