The Fractional Knapsack

In Post 21, I packed a hiking bag and greedy lost. "Best value per kilogram first" took the tent, ran out of room, and ended up with 38 when 48 was possible.

This post changes one rule. Instead of hiking gear, imagine you're filling a sack at a market where things are sold by weight: rice, lentils, flour, dried fruit. You can scoop out as much or as little of each as you like. Then the same greedy rule becomes not just good, but perfect.

I find this pair, 0/1 knapsack and fractional knapsack, the clearest example in the whole series of how one small rule change moves a problem from NP-Hard to easy.

The Fractional Version​

Fractional Knapsack: items have weights and values. You can take any fraction of an item, between 0 and all of it, getting that fraction of its value. Maximise total value with total weight at most WWW.

Here's the same bag and items as Post 21, but now you're allowed to take part of an item. Press Greedy by value per kg.

Greedy takes the whole tent (the best ratio, 5 per kg), leaving 4 kg of space. The next best ratio is the sleeping bag at 4.8 per kg, but it weighs 5 kg. In Post 21, greedy had to skip it. Now it takes four-fifths of it, exactly filling the bag.

Value: 30+45×24=30+19.2=49.230 + \frac{4}{5} \times 24 = 30 + 19.2 = 49.230+54​×24=30+19.2=49.2.

That's the best possible with fractions allowed. And notice it's more than 48, the best possible with whole items only. Keep that in mind; it matters later.

The Algorithm​

def fractional_knapsack(items, capacity):
    """items: list of (weight, value). Returns the best total value."""
    total = 0.0
    # best value per kilogram first
    for weight, value in sorted(items, key=lambda it: it[1] / it[0], reverse=True):
        if capacity <= 0:
            break
        take = min(weight, capacity)          # all of it, or whatever fits
        total += value * (take / weight)
        capacity -= take
    return total

bag = [(6, 30), (5, 24), (5, 24), (1, 3), (2, 5)]
print(fractional_knapsack(bag, 10))   # 49.2

Sort once, then a single pass. That's about nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn steps, which is fast for any size.

Why Greedy Is Perfect Here​

The proof is another exchange argument, like the meeting room in Post 68.

Suppose some packing is better than greedy's. Then it must be using some weight on an item with a lower value per kilogram, while leaving some of a higher value-per-kilogram item behind (otherwise it would be the greedy packing).

Take a small amount of weight away from the low-ratio item, and put exactly the same amount of weight of the high-ratio item in. The total weight doesn't change. The total value goes up, because each kilogram moved is now worth more.

So that "better" packing wasn't the best after all: it could be improved. The only packing that can't be improved this way is the one where every kilogram is filled in ratio order, which is exactly what greedy does.

And why it fails for 0/1​

That proof only works because you can move a small amount of weight. With whole items, you can't take "a bit" of the tent out and put "a bit" of the sleeping bag in. Swapping whole items changes the weight by large, uneven amounts, and the neat argument falls apart. The tent's 6 kg left an awkward 4 kg gap that no remaining whole item filled well.

All of the difficulty of 0/1 knapsack lives in that indivisibility.

The Real Point: Relaxation Gives a Bound​

Here's why this little problem matters far beyond markets.

The fractional problem is a relaxation of the 0/1 problem: it allows everything the 0/1 problem allows (whole items are just fractions of 0 or 1), plus more. So its best answer is at least as good as the 0/1 best answer:

best with whole items≤best with fractions\text{best with whole items} \leq \text{best with fractions}best with whole items≤best with fractions

On our bag: 48≤49.248 \leq 49.248≤49.2. We can compute 49.2 instantly with greedy. So without solving the 0/1 problem at all, we know: nobody can pack whole items worth more than 49.2.

That's exactly the "bound" idea from Post 62. It's what lets a solver say "my answer is within 2% of optimal" without knowing the optimum. And it's the engine of branch and bound from Post 63:

  • Branch
  • Bound
  • Prune

To solve 0/1 knapsack exactly, search through decisions: is the tent in or out? Is the sleeping bag in or out? That's a tree with 2n2^n2n leaves.

At each point in the search, some items are decided and the rest aren't. Solve the fractional knapsack on the undecided items, with the remaining space. That's fast, and it gives the best value this branch could possibly reach.

If that bound is no better than a whole-item packing you've already found, skip the entire branch. Nothing inside it can beat what you have.

On real instances, this skips almost all of the 2n2^n2n leaves. The worst case is still exponential, but typical cases become fast.

The same idea, with fractional versions of much bigger problems solved by linear programming, is how the airline crew problems in Post 55 and the huge TSP instances in Post 20 get solved. Fractions are meaningless as answers (you can't fly 70% of a crew pairing), but they're extremely useful as bounds.

What We Have Established

In fractional knapsack, you can take any fraction of an item. Greedy by value per kilogram is optimal: on our bag it takes the tent and 80% of the sleeping bag for 49.2. The proof is an exchange argument: moving a little weight from a lower-ratio item to a higher-ratio one always increases value. It fails for 0/1 knapsack because whole items can't be split.

The fractional version is a relaxation, so its answer (49.2) bounds the 0/1 answer (48). Fast relaxations giving bounds are the engine of branch and bound and of the linear programming methods behind real-world exact solvers.
