Why Haven't We Solved It?

Welcome to Phase 4. This is the phase I was most nervous about writing, and the one I've been looking forward to most.

When I started this project, I had a secret assumption that I'm a bit embarrassed by now. I assumed P vs NP was unsolved mostly because it was hard, in the normal sense: a long, complicated proof that nobody had quite managed to finish. Like a very difficult exam question that just needs more working.

That's not the situation at all. The real situation is stranger, and honestly more interesting. It's not that people have run out of ideas. It's that people have proved that many of the best ideas cannot work. There are theorems about why the other theorems don't exist.

This post is the overview. The next fifteen go through it piece by piece.

Two Kinds of Results​

Every result about how hard a problem is falls into one of two kinds.

  • Upper bounds
  • Lower bounds

An upper bound says: "this problem can be solved in at most this much time".

To prove one, you write down one algorithm and show it works and runs fast enough. That's it. All of Phase 2's "it's in NP\mathcal{NP}NP" arguments were upper bounds. So is every algorithm you've ever been taught.

Upper bounds are about existence: there exists an algorithm.

A lower bound says: "this problem cannot be solved in less than this much time, by any algorithm".

To prove one, you have to rule out every algorithm that could ever be written. Not just the ones people have tried. Not just the ones that look sensible. Every possible sequence of instructions, including ones nobody will ever think of.

Lower bounds are about impossibility: there does not exist an algorithm.

P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP is a lower bound. It says: there is no polynomial-time algorithm for SAT. To prove it, you'd have to rule out infinitely many possible algorithms at once.

Post 10 already mentioned this asymmetry. Phase 4 is about why it's so severe.

How Bad Our Lower Bounds Are​

Here's the part that genuinely shocked me when I first read it.

It's not just that we can't prove SAT needs exponential time. On realistic models of computers, nobody has even proved that SAT needs substantially more than linear time, meaning about as long as it takes to read the formula once.

Think about that. SAT is believed to need something like 2n2^n2n steps in the worst case. The best proven lower bound for general algorithms is barely more than "you have to at least read the input". The gap between what we believe and what we can prove is almost the entire universe of possible running times.

It's the same story with circuits, which we'll meet properly in Post 39. Most functions need enormous circuits; Claude Shannon proved that in 1949 by counting. But if you pick a specific function in NP\mathcal{NP}NP and ask for a proof that it needs big circuits, the best lower bound anyone has ever proved, for any such function, is only a little over 3n3n3n gates, where nnn is the number of inputs.

We know hard problems are everywhere. We can almost never prove that a particular one is hard.

What a Barrier Is​

A barrier is a theorem that says: "any proof built in this particular way can never settle P vs NP".

It doesn't say P vs NP is unsolvable. It says a certain kind of argument is a dead end. It's like a map that marks entire regions with "no path through here". You still don't know where the path is, but you know where it isn't.

There are three major barriers, and Phase 4 explains each one:

BarrierYearByWhat it rules out
Relativization1975Theodore Baker, John Gill, Robert SolovayArguments that would still work if every computer had the same magic "oracle" bolted on, which includes simple diagonalization
Natural Proofs1994Alexander Razborov, Steven RudichA broad family of arguments that find a simple property that hard functions have, assuming strong cryptography exists
Algebrization2008Scott Aaronson, Avi WigdersonAn upgraded version of relativization, covering the algebraic techniques that got past the first barrier

Each barrier was a response to a burst of progress. Someone develops a powerful new technique, it proves impressive new results, people get excited that it might crack P vs NP, and then someone proves that it can't.

Is It Even Provable?​

When I tell people about the barriers, the question I get most often is: "maybe P vs NP is just unprovable?"

It's a fair question. In 1931, Kurt Gödel showed that in any reasonable system of mathematical axioms, there are true statements that can't be proved from those axioms. And there are real examples: the Continuum Hypothesis, a question about the sizes of infinite sets, was shown to be neither provable nor disprovable from the standard axioms of mathematics.

Could P vs NP be like that? Nobody has ruled it out. But very few researchers think it's likely. Most people believe P vs NP has a definite answer that can be proved; we just don't have the tools yet.

I like this because it connects to what I wrote in the founder blog when I started Azeon: I think the answer already exists, and the problem is the gap between where we are and where the answer lives. The barriers are a map of that gap.

What We Have Established

P vs NP is unsolved not for lack of effort, but because proving a lower bound (that no algorithm exists) is fundamentally harder than proving an upper bound (that one does). Our current lower bounds are extremely weak: on general models, nothing much beyond linear time for SAT.

Three barriers (relativization in 1975, natural proofs in 1994, algebrization in 2008) are theorems proving that entire families of proof techniques cannot settle P vs NP. A successful proof must avoid all of them.
