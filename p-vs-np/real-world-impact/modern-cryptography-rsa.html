Modern Cryptography (RSA)

Every time you see the little padlock next to a website's address, two computers that have never met before have agreed on a secret, in public, with anyone on the network able to watch. That still sounds impossible to me, even after learning how it works.

For decades, the most famous tool for that kind of public-key cryptography has been RSA. This post explains how it works with tiny numbers, connects it to P vs NP, and corrects a claim I used to repeat that turns out to be too strong.

The Problem RSA Solves​

Old-fashioned encryption uses one secret key to lock and unlock. That works if you can meet in person to share the key. It doesn't work when your laptop wants to talk securely to a bank's server for the first time.

Public-key cryptography uses two keys:

  • a public key, which you give to everyone, and which anyone can use to lock a message to you,
  • a private key, which only you have, and which is the only thing that can unlock it.

It's like a padlock you hand out freely. Anyone can snap it shut on a box. Only you have the key that opens it.

The idea of public-key cryptography was published by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976. A year later, Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman found a practical way to do it, published in 1978: RSA, from their initials. (It later came out that Clifford Cocks, at the British intelligence agency GCHQ, had found essentially the same idea in 1973, but it was kept secret until 1997.)

How RSA Works​

You need the clock arithmetic ( mod \bmodmod) from Post 31.

  • 1. Make keys
  • 2. Lock
  • 3. Unlock
  • Pick two different prime numbers, ppp and qqq. Keep them secret.
  • Multiply them: n=p×qn = p \times qn=p×q. Publish nnn.
  • Compute φ=(p−1)(q−1)\varphi = (p - 1)(q - 1)φ=(p−1)(q−1). Keep it secret.
  • Pick a small number eee that shares no factor with φ\varphiφ. Publish eee.
  • Compute ddd, the number with e×d mod φ=1e \times d \bmod \varphi = 1e×dmodφ=1. This uses Euclid's method from Post 33 and is fast, if you know φ\varphiφ. Keep ddd secret.

Public key: (n,e)(n, e)(n,e). Private key: ddd.

To send a message mmm (a number smaller than nnn), compute:

c=me mod nc = m^e \bmod nc=memodn

and send ccc. That's the locked message.

The owner computes:

cd mod nc^d \bmod ncdmodn

and gets mmm back. A theorem from number theory (Euler's theorem, a generalisation of Fermat's from Post 31) guarantees this always works.

Here's the whole thing with small primes. Pick ppp, qqq and a message, and watch the locked and unlocked values.

Look at the status line. With these tiny numbers, an attacker who sees only nnn can split it into p×qp \times qp×q in a few dozen divisions, then compute ddd themselves, exactly the way the owner did. Toy RSA is completely insecure.

Real RSA uses primes with hundreds of digits each, so nnn has about 600 digits or more (2048 bits is the usual minimum today). Trial division would need more steps than there are atoms in the universe. Far better factoring methods exist, but the best of them still takes time that grows faster than any polynomial in the number of digits.

Where the Security Comes From​

The whole scheme comes down to one asymmetry:

  • Multiplying p×qp \times qp×q is easy, even for 300-digit numbers.
  • Factoring nnn back into ppp and qqq seems to be extremely hard.

As far as anyone knows publicly, the only practical way to find ddd from (n,e)(n, e)(n,e) is to factor nnn. The largest RSA challenge number factored so far, RSA-250 (829 bits), was factored in 2020 by a team of researchers using thousands of core-years of computing time. 2048-bit RSA is far beyond that.

So RSA's safety rests on factoring being hard. Which brings us to P vs NP.

The Correction: "RSA Depends on P ≠ NP"​

I've seen this sentence many times, and I wrote something like it in Post 10. It's partly true. Here's the careful version.

  • What's true
  • What's not true

Factoring is in NP\mathcal{NP}NP: if someone gives you ppp and qqq, you multiply them and check. So if P=NP\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}P=NP, factoring would be solvable in polynomial time, and RSA would be broken (at least in principle, depending on how fast that polynomial algorithm actually is).

So P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP is necessary for RSA to be secure.

But P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP is not enough. Three separate gaps:

  1. Factoring might be easy even if P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP. Post 25 showed factoring is in NP∩coNP\mathcal{NP} \cap co\mathcal{NP}NP∩coNP and is believed not to be NP-Complete. So P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP only says the hardest problems in NP\mathcal{NP}NP are hard. Factoring isn't one of them. Someone could find a fast factoring algorithm without touching P vs NP at all.

  2. Worst-case versus typical. P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP says some instances are hard. RSA needs the specific nnn you generated to be hard. That's a much stronger demand, about typical cases. Remember the knapsack encryption from Post 21 that was broken for exactly this reason.

  3. Quantum computers. Shor's algorithm (Post 33) factors in polynomial time on a quantum computer. That has nothing to do with P\mathcal{P}P vs NP\mathcal{NP}NP; it's about BQP\mathcal{BQP}BQP. A large quantum computer would break RSA even if P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP is proved tomorrow.

So the honest statement is: RSA's security requires P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP, but also requires much more, and that "more" is not proven.

This is true for all of modern public-key cryptography. It rests on specific hardness assumptions, about factoring, discrete logarithms or lattices, that are stronger than P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP and are supported by decades of failed attacks, not by proofs. Post 41 described the general version: cryptography needs one-way functions, and their existence implies P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP, not the other way round.

RSA Today​

A few things worth knowing about how this is actually used now:

  • Most secure connections today agree on their session keys using elliptic curve cryptography rather than RSA, because it's faster with smaller keys. RSA is still very widely used for signatures and certificates.
  • Both RSA and elliptic curve cryptography are broken by Shor's algorithm. That's why, as Post 33 mentioned, new post-quantum standards were published in 2024, and browsers and messaging apps have already started using them alongside the older methods.
  • Real RSA also adds padding: random bits mixed into the message before encryption. Without it, even with huge primes, "textbook RSA" like the figure above has serious weaknesses.
What We Have Established

Public-key cryptography uses a public key to lock and a private key to unlock. RSA (Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, 1977) publishes n=p×qn = p \times qn=p×q and eee; locking is me mod nm^e \bmod nmemodn, unlocking is cd mod nc^d \bmod ncdmodn, and computing ddd requires knowing ppp and qqq. Security rests on factoring large nnn being hard. The largest RSA challenge number factored so far has 829 bits; 2048 bits is the usual minimum.

P≠NP\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}P=NP is necessary for RSA but not sufficient: factoring is probably not NP-Complete, encryption needs typical-case hardness, and quantum computers break RSA regardless of P vs NP.
