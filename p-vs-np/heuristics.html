Phase 6: Heuristics

If we can't solve NP-Hard problems perfectly, what do engineers actually do? This phase covers the practical toolkit: approximation algorithms, greedy strategies, dynamic programming, local search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and modern SAT solvers. This is where theory meets engineering.

📄️ Coping with NP-Hardness

So your problem is NP-Hard. Now what? Engineers don't give up; they choose what to give up. Exactness, speed on every input, or generality: pick which one to sacrifice, and a whole toolbox opens.

📄️ Approximation Algorithms

You can't find the best answer quickly, but you can sometimes prove your quick answer is never worse than twice the best, without ever knowing what the best is. Some problems allow amazing guarantees. Others allow none at all.

📄️ Approximating Vertex Cover

Back to the cameras from Posts 11 and 16. Pick any unwatched road and put cameras on both ends. Repeat. That's the whole algorithm, it's never worse than twice the best, and the proof fits in four lines.

📄️ TSP and the Triangle Inequality

General TSP can't be approximated at all. Add one sensible rule, that going direct is never longer than taking a detour, and a spanning tree gives a tour within twice the best. Christofides' 1976 trick gets that to 1.5.

📄️ PTAS and FPTAS

For some NP-Hard problems you get to choose: name any accuracy you like, say within 1% of the best, and there's a polynomial-time algorithm that achieves it. The price is time. Knapsack is the classic example, and it uses a rounding trick I love.

📄️ Greedy Algorithms

Take the best-looking option right now, never look back. It's the simplest strategy there is. Sometimes it's provably perfect, sometimes it's badly wrong, and learning to tell which is one of the most useful skills in algorithm design.

📄️ The Fractional Knapsack

Same bag, same items, one rule changed: you may take part of an item. The greedy strategy that failed in Post 21 becomes perfect, and the reason explains why relaxing a hard problem gives you a bound on its answer.

📄️ Dynamic Programming Limits

Dynamic programming solves knapsack exactly with a simple table. It looks polynomial. It isn't, and watching the table grow one digit at a time shows exactly why pseudo-polynomial time breaks down for big numbers.

📄️ Local Search Algorithms

Start with any solution, even a terrible random one. Make a small change. Keep it if it helps. Repeat until nothing helps. That's local search, it powers a huge amount of real-world optimisation, and its one big weakness is getting stuck.
