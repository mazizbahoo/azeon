# Summary of the Barriers

Sixteen posts ago, I said Phase 4 was the one I was most nervous about. Now that I've written it, I think it's the phase that changed my understanding the most.

At the start, I thought P vs NP was unsolved because it was a hard problem. I now think it's unsolved because it's a **different kind** of problem from almost everything mathematicians have ever proved. This post puts the whole picture in one place, and then ends on the most hopeful result I found.

## The Three Barriers, Side by Side

| | Relativization | Natural Proofs | Algebrization |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year** | 1975 | 1994 | 2008 |
| **By** | Baker, Gill, Solovay | Razborov, Rudich | Aaronson, Wigderson |
| **Post** | 37 | 40, 41 | 43 |
| **Blocks** | Arguments that work unchanged when every machine has an oracle | Circuit lower bounds via a property that's useful, large and easy to check | Arguments that still work when machines can query a polynomial extension of an oracle |
| **Main victims** | Diagonalization and simulation | Essentially every circuit lower bound technique known in 1994 | Arithmetization, the method behind $\mathcal{IP} = \mathcal{PSPACE}$ |
| **Condition** | None, it's unconditional | Assumes strong pseudorandom functions exist | None, it's unconditional |
| **Escape route** | Look inside the machine | Use a rare or hard-to-check property | Go beyond polynomial extensions |

And the more specific no-go results:

- **Linear programs for TSP** must be exponentially large (Yannakakis 1988; Fiorini et al. 2012). Post 48.
- **Occurrence obstructions** can't power GCT's approach to permanent versus determinant (Bürgisser, Ikenmeyer, Panova 2016). Post 45.
- **Resolution proofs** of some tautologies, like the pigeonhole principle, must be exponentially long (Haken 1985). Post 26.

## What Each Barrier Teaches

I think each barrier is best understood as a lesson about what a proof must contain.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="rel" label="Relativization" default>

**A proof must use something specific about real computation.**

Treating programs as black boxes that can be listed and simulated isn't enough, because that view can't tell the difference between our world and an oracle world where $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}$.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="nat" label="Natural Proofs">

**A proof must use something specific about the hard function.**

A property that most random functions share can't work, because cryptography makes simple functions look random. The proof needs to say something about SAT, or NP-Complete problems, that isn't just "it looks complicated".

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="alg" label="Algebrization">

**Looking inside with polynomials isn't enough either.**

The algebraic tricks that beat relativization are themselves blind to the difference between worlds where $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{NP}$ and worlds where it isn't. The proof needs something beyond extending computations into polynomials.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

Put together: a successful proof must look inside computation, in a way that's specific to the hard problem, and goes beyond algebraic extension. Very few known techniques do all three.

## The Hopeful Part: Williams, 2011

I promised a hopeful ending, and here it is.

For decades, circuit lower bounds had been stuck at a frustratingly low level. One class in particular, called $\mathcal{ACC}^0$, was a famous embarrassment. It's a class of shallow circuits, like $\mathcal{AC}^0$ from Post 39, but also allowed to use gates that count modulo some number. Researchers couldn't even prove that $\mathcal{NEXP}$, the nondeterministic version of $\mathcal{EXP}$ and an absolutely enormous class, wasn't contained in it.

In 2011, Ryan Williams proved:

$$\mathcal{NEXP} \not\subseteq \mathcal{ACC}^0$$

The result itself is far weaker than $\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}$. What made it famous was **how** it was proved.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="alg" label="Step 1: a better algorithm" default>

Williams found a slightly faster-than-brute-force algorithm for a satisfiability question about $\mathcal{ACC}^0$ circuits. Not polynomial, just a little better than trying all $2^n$ inputs.

That's an **upper bound**: an algorithm that exists.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="turn" label="Step 2: turn it into a lower bound">

Then he showed that if $\mathcal{NEXP}$ **were** inside $\mathcal{ACC}^0$, his faster algorithm could be used to speed up **every** nondeterministic exponential-time computation by a little.

But the nondeterministic time hierarchy theorem (Post 47, proved by diagonalization) says that can't happen: more nondeterministic time genuinely solves more problems.

So $\mathcal{NEXP}$ can't be inside $\mathcal{ACC}^0$.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="why" label="Why it got past the barriers">

The proof combines a diagonalization (which relativizes on its own) with an algorithm that looks deep inside the structure of $\mathcal{ACC}^0$ circuits (which doesn't). The combination doesn't relativize.

It also doesn't work by finding a large, easy-to-check property of hard functions, so it doesn't fit the natural proofs template. And it isn't an arithmetization argument of the kind the algebrization barrier was built around.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

The big idea, sometimes called the **algorithmic method**, is that **faster algorithms imply lower bounds**. Finding a clever upper bound can prove an impossibility. That turned the usual relationship between algorithms and complexity upside down, and since 2011, researchers have used it to push further, including a 2018 result by Cody Murray and Ryan Williams that strengthened the theorem to a smaller class than $\mathcal{NEXP}$.

It doesn't come close to P vs NP. But it's a proof that the barriers are not walls around the whole field. They're walls around specific roads, and new roads can be built.

## Does It Need New Mathematics?

The spreadsheet I planned this series with had a note next to this post: "Synthesize why a completely new branch of mathematics may be required." Having written Phase 4, here's my honest version of that claim.

It's not proven that new mathematics is required. But consider what we know:

- The three barriers rule out the main techniques that complexity theory had developed from the 1960s to the 2000s.
- The most ambitious program trying to escape them, GCT, needs deep results in algebraic geometry and representation theory that mathematicians don't have yet.
- The one clean recent success, Williams' theorem, needed a genuinely new idea, and still only reached a much weaker result.

Many researchers have said, in various ways, that they expect a proof of $\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}$ to require ideas we can't currently imagine. I think that's the most reasonable reading of the evidence. Not "it's impossible", but "it needs a kind of understanding of computation that nobody has had yet".

That, honestly, is what makes it exciting to me. The answer is out there. The map of where it isn't keeps getting more detailed.

:::note What We Have Established
Three barriers block the main known techniques for settling P vs NP: **relativization** (1975) blocks black-box simulation and diagonalization; **natural proofs** (1994) blocks circuit arguments based on large, easy-to-check properties, assuming strong cryptography; **algebrization** (2008) blocks the polynomial-extension methods that beat relativization. Specific no-go results also rule out polynomial-size LPs for TSP and occurrence obstructions in GCT.

Ryan Williams' 2011 proof that $\mathcal{NEXP} \not\subseteq \mathcal{ACC}^0$ combined a faster algorithm with diagonalization to get past the known barriers for a weaker question. It shows the barriers block specific roads, not all roads. A proof of $\mathcal{P} \neq \mathcal{NP}$ seems to need ideas that don't exist yet.
:::
